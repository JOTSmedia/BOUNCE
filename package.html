BOUNCE

Development package • working draft

An original ensemble musical for Broadway and a feature-film adaptation.

Working tagline: EVERY FALL HAS A RHYTHM.

Working setting: Las Vegas at the opening, then contemporary New York City, August through December. Zay’s Las Vegas upbringing and lifelong New York Knicks ambition are part of the story’s foundation. Supporting names and later plot choices remain development proposals. The Desert Star cabaret and Lake Erie Forge are fictional; the Forge is an independent professional opportunity with no NBA affiliation.

Basketball development note: The New York Knicks are Zay’s dream. His first New York attempt is a local tryout for their NBA G League affiliate, Westchester, rather than a walk-in NBA audition at Madison Square Garden. Local tryouts can lead to training-camp invitations, not guaranteed contracts; Westchester has carried local-tryout players onto its roster. The dramatic season and its dates are fictional, not an announcement of a real tryout. See the NBA G League’s local-tryout explanation and Westchester’s account of its 2025–26 roster.

1. Logline and one-page synopsis

Logline

After years supporting his mother in Las Vegas, a gifted 6'8" basketball hopeful follows his Knicks dream to New York—then jeopardizes seven artists’ trust by turning their private lives into his inspirational publicity. To earn a professional chance, he must stop measuring love by sacrifice and rebuild the support he has mistaken for an audience.

Synopsis

In darkness, a basketball meets a kick drum. Isaiah “Zay” Reed, twenty-six and 6'8", makes gravity look like a game. Seven artists join him in distinct pools of light. Almost-triumph snaps into a cramped Las Vegas kitchen: his mother, Denise, demands money and humiliates his ambition. He leaves with a ball and a small bag.

Denise is a cabaret server whose alcohol addiction has damaged work, money, and relationships. Her boyfriends were always “Uncle.” Ray Walker was the one who stayed in Zay’s life after marrying and divorcing her. Unable to remain her partner, he kept coaching the boy he believed in. At eighteen, just after graduation and as a college basketball scholarship became possible, Zay’s absent biological father died. Papers revealed that his father had submitted grant applications for him. Grief, the divorce, and fear of leaving Denise without support made Zay turn down the college route and available aid. He worked, trained with Ray, and saved for eight years.

Now his New York flight is paid for; somewhere to sleep is not settled. Ray drives him to the airport without promising a rescue. The next evening Zay finds childhood Las Vegas friend Dani at The Switch, a New York bar and cabaret. There he meets Lena, a ballet-trained exotic dancer. An intimate duet breaks when he says she could be a “real dancer again.” She orders him out. Someone whose ambition was devalued has devalued another person’s work.

Dani and roommate Ben offer three nights, then negotiate a paid arrangement. Ray provides training records and a coach introduction. Coach Simone Vale prepares Zay for a September Westchester Knicks local tryout. He earns no camp invitation. She helps him examine the film and pursue the fictional Lake Erie Forge’s November 15 application and December Buffalo camp. The Knicks remain the dream; the daily work is defense, passing, conditioning, shifts, and receiving correction.

The others pursue independent ambitions. Ben’s gallery job connects Iris to a court-side mural commission; she paints Zay without his heroic pose. Lena and Morgan develop a paid movement-and-music premiere. Velvet hires lead guitarist Jude, whose solos compete with the act. Dani betrays Ben’s confidence for a laugh. Morgan lands a film-scoring offer while avoiding a credit dispute with Jude.

A sponsor offers camp travel in exchange for Rise of Reed. Zay approves private rehearsal footage, Iris’s unfinished portrait, Dani’s harmful joke, and other people’s disclosures as evidence of his inspirational journey. Morgan licenses a rough collaborative cue without permission. Attention arrives; trust fractures. Act I leaves Zay enormous on a screen and alone beneath it.

Repair costs publicity, money, and time. Zay withdraws the film and loses the sponsor. Morgan returns the fee and negotiates proper credit. Dani apologizes without a punchline and rewrites the audition. Iris insists on an honest image license. Velvet refuses a lucrative caricature booking. Ben obtains clear acting terms. Lena requires correction before inviting Zay into a limited role in her piece. Ray helps review possessions, but will not refill Zay’s budget or make Denise’s recovery his responsibility.

A December 14 camp callback conflicts with the premiere. Zay discloses it, pays for rehearsed cover, and asks for release. The show proceeds without him. At camp, he accepts correction and makes the extra pass. Days later comes a six-week paid Forge contract—a first professional step, with the NBA still far away. The others gain work and firmer boundaries at different speeds. Denise remains responsible for her own recovery. Zay can love her without moving back. In the finale, he leaves space between impacts. Eight distinct colors hold; someone else can carry the beat.

2. Character bible

All principals are adults. Ages, identities, and relationship configurations are working choices for casting and development. The seven familiar chakra colors form a theatrical design vocabulary; pearl-white Soul Star is BOUNCE’s artistic eighth extension, not a claim that this eight-part scheme is a universally established traditional system. Color describes pressure and change rather than personality type or spiritual attainment.

Isaiah “Zay” Reed • 26 • professional basketball hopeful • solar plexus / yellow

Ambition and craft. Born and raised in Las Vegas, Zay has wanted to play for the New York Knicks since childhood. At 6'8", he can defend several positions and see over a trap, but he overdribbles when he feels watched. Years of warehouse and event shifts fund training, family bills, and finally a New York flight and local-tryout budget. He has continued competitive adult-league play and supervised work with Ray; his talent has not survived on wish alone. His September Westchester Knicks G League tryout does not produce a camp invitation. Vale helps him turn that disappointment into a plan for current, continuous game film and the independent Forge opportunity. Show possession labels, closeout angles, weak-side rotations, free throws while tired, and a smaller offensive role. Madison Square Garden is a destination he imagines, not the location of an open NBA audition.

Upbringing and coping. Denise, a single mother and server at Las Vegas’s fictional Desert Star cabaret, could remember a table’s entire order and save for Zay’s shoes, then drink away money they needed and make tenderness conditional on his usefulness. Each new boyfriend was introduced as “Uncle”; Zay learned to attach quickly and pack his feelings when a man disappeared. Ray Walker married Denise, then divorced her when he could no longer participate in the relationship amid her addiction. He kept his commitment to Zay: practices, film, transport, applications. Zay calls him Uncle Ray, never mistakes him for a blood relative, and mistrusts every missed call as a possible ending. He is funny about his height, playful with children, and quietly excellent at repairing household objects.

The college choice. Zay finished high school at eighteen. That summer, while a basketball scholarship route was coming together, his long-absent biological father died. Among the papers were copies of grant applications his father had submitted on his behalf, alongside an unfinished form for another fund. Applications were not awards, and the late gesture did not undo the years he was absent. A coach and aid office had made a possible college package concrete, but grief and Denise’s recent divorce from Ray convinced Zay that leaving would abandon his family. He declined the college place and available support, took more paid shifts, and stayed. The story respects that constrained choice without calling it destiny or the only responsible option. He carries anger both at the father who did not show up and at himself for the life he did not take.

Flaw. He hears correction as a verdict on his worth. He reproduces the hierarchy that hurts him: paid over unpaid, professional over amateur, respectable over embarrassing. He thinks giving people exposure is generosity even when it removes their choices.

Relationships. Dani knew him in their Las Vegas neighborhood and moved to New York earlier; Ben insists that a couch has a calendar. Lena resists becoming proof that he is a good man. Iris sees him accurately. Vale evaluates choices, not mythology. Ray is a chosen mentor with limits, not an unlimited father-shaped resource. Denise remains someone he loves and cannot safely live with; care does not require surrendering his income, location, or future.

Decisive choice and cost. He authorizes withdrawal of Rise of Reed before knowing whether anyone will help replace the lost travel money. Later he asks to be released from his performance commitment, arranges paid cover, and accepts that the answer could be no.

Progress. A six-week paid injury-cover Forge contract, with no promise of renewal or NBA affiliation. The Knicks dream stays alive without a miraculous call-up. He rents a modest room, contributes a fixed affordable amount to Denise without accepting unrestricted demands, and retains a training plan. He thanks Ray for specific help rather than asking whether Ray will stay forever. Yellow loses its brittle, overexposed edge; his body occupies space without demanding every eye.

Supporting family roles and chronology

Denise Reed — mother and Las Vegas cabaret server. A skilled, funny worker who knows regulars and protects a new server from a rude customer, she also misses shifts, lies about drinking, and humiliates her son. The profession does not cause the addiction and the addiction does not excuse the abuse. Her concrete desire is to keep her job and apartment. Her independent late-story action is making her own call about support and requesting a shift arrangement; neither sobriety nor reconciliation is certified by the finale. Zay cannot do recovery on her behalf.

Ray “Uncle Ray” Walker — former stepfather, coach, and mentor. One of the men called Uncle became a temporary father figure and did not discard Zay when the marriage ended. Ray’s early help includes practice plans, reliable rides, game-film records, and introducing Zay to Vale. He has work and obligations of his own. He refuses to mediate the old marriage, promises no roster place, and cannot keep paying emergencies. He once mistook Zay’s silence for agreement; he now asks before giving advice. His decisive act is telling Zay that choosing his own future is compatible with loving Denise, then remaining reachable after Zay actually leaves.

Biological father — absent, later deceased. He is present through papers and Zay’s conflicting memories, not a redemptive ghost. His late grant effort creates a question the son cannot ask him; it does not prove a secret lifetime of good parenting.

Timeline to protect in every version: High-school graduation at eighteen → biological father’s death as college scholarship plans mature → discovery of grant paperwork, with Denise and Ray already divorced → Zay declines college/available aid and stays to earn → eight years of paid work, family support, training, and saving → Las Vegas departure at twenty-six → New York arrival and September G League local tryout → November 15 Forge film deadline → December 12–14 camp/premiere conflict. The eight principal chakra identities remain unchanged; these family roles do not add a ninth symbolic identity.

Lena Marlow • 28 • ballet-trained dancer and working exotic dancer • sacral / orange

Ambition and craft. Lena wants a paid, self-directed movement piece. Ballet, floorwork, club dancing, balance, improvisation, and managing an audience are all in her vocabulary. She knows which shoes survive which floors, negotiates bookings, and can make a room laugh with a single held balance. She enjoys desire and attention when she has chosen their terms.

Upbringing and coping. A family proud of her discipline outsourced emotional approval to teachers and casting decisions. A training culture taught her to monitor her body as an object. When tuition assistance ended and a rent shortfall made unpaid ballet work unsustainable, a colleague introduced her to paid exotic dancing; financial urgency opened that door, while her present choices reflect skills, pleasures, and terms she has made her own. She now protects herself with competence and quick judgments about whose interest is safe.

Flaw. She sometimes assumes that asking for help means surrendering control. She may cancel a promising collaboration rather than negotiate a boundary, leaving other people to guess what she needed.

Relationships. Velvet exchanges rehearsal space and teaches her audience control; she helps Velvet find movement sustainable across a full set. Morgan is a creative partner, with choreography credit and a share of the project fee. Zay becomes a possible friend and attraction only after the bar incident is addressed.

Decisive choice and cost. She declines a better-paid booking that would give a promoter ownership of new choreography and keeps selected club shifts to finance rehearsal. She requires Morgan to amend the cue agreement and Zay to remove her footage before continuing with either.

Progress. A paid premiere, a clean recording license, and a proposal for a second engagement. She continues exotic dance by choice while developing choreography; ballet is a living resource rather than a status she must reclaim. She initiates the later invitation to Zay: coffee, no audience, no promise. Orange carries pleasure as well as injury.

Dani Price • 27 • comedian • throat / blue

Ambition and craft. Dani wants a four-week paid opening slot with a regional touring comic, beginning in January. They keep a notebook of tags, time sets, cut material that drags, and rewrite transitions after listening to recordings. Their comic eye catches the bureaucratic absurdities of city survival.

Upbringing and coping. Dani grew up reading raised voices before understanding the argument. A joke could interrupt danger. Now a laugh can feel like proof that a room is safe, even when the person being laughed at is not.

Flaw. Dani turns confidences into bits before checking whose experience is being used. Their joke about Ben knowing how to pack before anyone asks him to leave is skilled, revealing, and a betrayal. Calling it “truth” does not make it theirs to sell.

Relationships. Zay is a childhood friend from Las Vegas; Dani moved to New York before him and knows how much it cost him to buy the flight. He is not a new rescue project. Ben is roommate and rehearsal partner with an independent right to privacy. Velvet eventually hires Dani to host the headline set, with a clear fee and no personal material about the company.

Decisive choice and cost. Dani cuts the strongest laugh in the audition set, requests removal of a posted clip, and accepts that the booker must see a new tape. In silence at the apartment table, they tell Ben: “You trusted me with that. I used it. You don’t have to make me feel better.” They do not add a joke.

Progress. The paid opening engagement comes after a second audition, not from notoriety. Ben resumes rehearsing with them under explicit limits; trust is partial. Blue grows clearer when Dani stops using volume and wit to fill every pause.

Andre Bell / Velvet Saint • 30 • drag performer • crown / violet

Ambition and craft. Andre is a drag artist whose stage persona, Velvet Saint, marries live vocal authority, comic timing, costume engineering, and exact audience control. The goal is a headline show with a guaranteed fee, rehearsal pay, and approval over promotion. Andre tracks ticket sales, cues costume changes, and repairs a fastening while negotiating a contract.

Upbringing and coping. Acceptance depended on an approved public identity. Velvet is invention and pleasure, not simply armor; nevertheless Andre sometimes brings the command of the persona into private conversations where vulnerability would work better.

Flaw. Andre equates being indispensable with belonging. A tempting corporate booking offers good money for a flattened, insulting act. Taking it could finance the desired show while teaching everyone what can be bought.

Relationships. Lena is a colleague who can contradict Andre without asking permission. Jude is an employee whose talent must serve a complete theatrical event. Dani becomes a host rather than an unpaid friend filling a gap.

Decisive choice and cost. Andre declines the caricature brief after trying—and failing—to negotiate its terms. The loss means a smaller venue, a reused costume, and two fewer musical arrangements. Andre tells Lena plainly that the refusal hurts, instead of presenting it as effortless righteousness.

Progress. A modest headline run with a guaranteed minimum and a stronger live act. Andre learns to invite someone to dinner without making the invitation a performance. Violet connects separate people; it does not dissolve them into Velvet’s spotlight.

Ben Mercer • 25 • actor • root / red

Ambition and craft. Ben wants a meaningful role and enough stability to arrive prepared. He works temporary installation and event shifts, uses action verbs on scripts, records self-tapes against a borrowed wall, and listens closely when Dani gives him a line differently.

Upbringing and coping. Several unstable households taught Ben to discover the useful version of himself quickly. He is generous, observant, a good cook from whatever is left, and alarmingly able to say yes before hearing the question.

Flaw. Conflicting promises cause actual harm: he misses an installation call after covering a friend’s shift, leaving Iris to lift a panel she should not handle alone. His evasions are understandable but not harmless.

Relationships. Dani shares rent and rehearsal, then violates a confidence. Iris becomes a professional collaborator who asks him for a schedule he can honor. Zay learns from him that housing generosity requires consent from both residents.

Decisive choice and cost. Ben turns down an open-ended unpaid rehearsal request and asks for dates, a fee, and replacement terms. He risks being called difficult. He also admits an earlier missed call directly to Iris and completes paid repair work on an agreed schedule.

Progress. A small, paid role in a three-week new-play engagement, plus a predictable installation contract. He keeps his apartment share rather than abandoning it for a vague promise. Red becomes a place to stand rather than an alarm.

Jude Reyes • 29 • musician specializing in lead guitar • heart / green

Ambition and craft. Jude wants steady paid work and a sound recognizably his own. His vocabulary includes rock attack, funk restraint, and a singing, slightly delayed melodic turn. He understands tone, touring gear, and how to finish a difficult run; he is learning how to stop before the run.

Upbringing and coping. Inconsistent affection made emotional exposure feel like an invitation to withdrawal. Mastery offers a controlled form of intimacy. Jude cooks for people and leaves before they can thank him.

Flaw. Competition turns a collaborative cue into a solo opportunity. He claims that the music “speaks for itself,” conveniently avoiding a credit conversation until resentment has already entered the work.

Relationships. Morgan composes structure, harmony, and dramatic timing; Jude contributes a lead part and, where genuinely co-written, a melodic theme. Velvet can fire him for ignoring a cue. Lena helps him hear what a dancer needs between counts.

Decisive choice and cost. Jude agrees to a shorter featured solo and a documented division of composition and performance credit. He tells Morgan which phrase was his and which was not, and stays for the uncomfortable discussion instead of unplugging.

Progress. A paid live residency with Velvet and a sustainable scoring collaboration with Morgan. No label deal appears. Green moves from a virtuoso phrase held apart from the band to a phrase that answers another voice.

Iris Chen • 31 • painter • third eye / indigo

Ambition and craft. Iris wants a paid mural commission and a small exhibition that represent her own eye. She studies underpainting, tests pigments under the actual center lights, documents wall preparation, and separates a proposal image from a licensed finished work. Her dry humor is precise enough to feel like a small incision.

Upbringing and coping. Her household prized the appearance of calm over admitting what happened. She learned to make pleasing images and doubt uncomfortable perceptions. Now she can diagnose another person’s defenses while showing almost nothing of herself.

Flaw. She calls withholding “observation.” A portrait sitting with Zay risks becoming another extraction until she tells him what she sees, asks what he wishes to protect, and permits him to refuse a pose.

Relationships. Ben connects her to the center and becomes a collaborator whose labor she must value. Zay is a paid sitter within her commission budget, not free inspirational material. Morgan asks to project the portrait and must obtain permission like anyone else.

Decisive choice and cost. Iris rejects a sponsor’s request to remove the tired, unguarded hands from Zay’s image. She accepts a smaller approved production budget and negotiates a separate reproduction license. She also includes one small self-portrait in her gallery proposal, exposing her own unfinishedness.

Progress. The mural is paid in installments, with the final payment after installation acceptance. A two-person exhibition opens later; several paintings remain unsold. Indigo reveals surfaces and layers without giving Iris supernatural insight.

Morgan Ellis • 28 • songwriter, composer, and scorer • Soul Star / pearl white

Ambition and craft. Morgan wants properly credited composition work in film and theater. Songwriting means character, lyric, and melody; composing means form and harmonic architecture; scoring means meeting a picture’s timing and emotional need. Morgan can hear how fragments fit, but is also capable of writing a spare cue that belongs only to Morgan.

Upbringing and coping. A parent’s unrealized musical ambitions turned Morgan’s achievements into family compensation. Managing other people’s feelings became a practical skill. Morgan is funny about sound, meticulous with pencils, and secretly fond of a very unfashionable cadence.

Flaw. Morgan volunteers other people’s work as readily as their own time, mistaking collaboration for permission. Licensing the rough promo cue without clearing Jude’s recorded performance and Lena’s choreography is a consequential breach, even though Zay approves the film’s final cut.

Relationships. Jude challenges ownership and listening. Lena insists that movement has authorship. A short-film director offers an independent score with a real deadline; Morgan jeopardizes it by hiding delays while servicing everyone else.

Decisive choice and cost. Morgan withdraws the rough cue, returns the license fee, and names each contributor. They tell the film director the actual schedule, negotiate one fewer revision round, and risk losing the job instead of delivering an apology disguised as endless free work.

Progress. A completed, paid short-film score credited as “Original score by Morgan Ellis,” with Jude’s separately agreed credit for a co-written theme and guitar performance where applicable. The stage piece credits Lena’s choreography and shared concept explicitly. Pearl white makes room for the other colors without washing them away.

4. Two-act Broadway treatment

Working running time: Approximately 135 minutes including a fifteen-minute interval. A live band of keys, bass, drums/percussion, and guitar can expand with reeds or strings as resources allow. Jude’s featured playing must be credible; a visible instrumental double may support demanding passages without pretending the actor performs unavailable technique.

Stage grammar: One sprung central floor holds a court arc, a studio, and the open area of an apartment. Tracked frames and practical lamps create rooms without turning every location into a projection. The Switch is one workplace among several. Bench, barre, easel, folding table, guitar rig, and a door have specific dramatic jobs. No color substitutes for an action.

Act I • August to early November • the price of being seen

Opening / “BOUNCE.” A symbolic six-minute assembly gives each person pleasure and skill before biography. Zay establishes the groove alone. His routes remain clear as the others appear, and he catches the ball for demanding vocal phrases. Their individual desires overlap into an almost-resolved chorus. The kick stops. Denise’s exact household confrontation collapses the fantasy into a cramped Las Vegas kitchen. Her Desert Star server’s apron, a real overdue bill, and a concealed drink make the pressures specific. Zay leaves without a comeback speech, taking the small bag packed for a flight he has saved for and already booked.

The journey and The Switch / “AFTER THE APPLAUSE.” Keys, door, shoes, Las Vegas traffic, a brief call with Ray, an airport announcement, and a New York train carry one continuous rhythmic transition. Ray drives Zay to the red-eye but cannot give him a future on credit. A clear place-and-time shift makes the next evening’s arrival in New York literal. The Switch is not Denise’s workplace. Dani, who left their Las Vegas neighborhood earlier, is working. Lena initiates a conversation about Zay’s restless footwork, and a duet finds common ground in being watched. Zay’s “real dancer again” remark breaks it. She gives him a chance to explain, then orders him out. He obeys. The band does not sentimentalize his exit.

Three nights / “TERMS AND CONDITIONS.” In Dani and Ben’s apartment, a fitted sheet becomes a temporary sleeping place. Ben says “Of course” to a new guest before realizing what the week already contains. Dani stops the automatic agreement. They negotiate three nights, quiet hours, a key, and a check-in. Comedy comes from space: Zay’s feet occupy the route to the kettle. The number becomes a calendar of shifts, auditions, rent, and gym hours. Ben teaches Zay a load-in pattern of carrying, setting, and releasing weight; it later becomes transferable percussion.

A role, not a rescue. Ray sends continuous Las Vegas game film and introduces Zay to Coach Vale; she does not owe Ray a favorable answer. At the recreation center she watches Zay dominate a casual run, then lose a defensive assignment. She prepares him for a September Westchester local tryout. He storms out after “You were late on the rotation,” then returns the following morning and asks to see the possession again. At the tryout, gifted players expose the cost of his overdribbling. No camp invitation follows. Vale helps him build toward the independent Forge’s November 15 film deadline instead. This is a new plan, not a claim that the Forge feeds the Knicks. The return to work is small and imperfect. Meanwhile Ben’s gallery call introduces Iris to the administrator selecting the mural artist. Iris submits samples, a budget, and a schedule; the commission is not awarded as a favor.

The folder. After the tryout rejection, Zay empties his bag looking for an old stat sheet and finds copies of the college papers he kept. Dani recognizes the year. At the kitchen table he puts the scholarship correspondence beside his father’s unfinished grant form: “He found the office. Couldn’t find my gym.” Dani does not turn it into a joke or tell him the papers prove love. On a call, Ray acknowledges that he was moving out just as Zay needed adults to stay. “I thought staying home meant I was the man,” Zay says. Ray answers, “You were eighteen.” Zay folds the papers away and asks for the next practice time. The scene identifies grief and a sacrificed opportunity without treating his talent as untouched by the intervening years.

Movement and ownership / “WEIGHT OF MY OWN.” In a hired rehearsal room, Lena makes a ballet preparation change direction into her own floorwork. Morgan tries to label the movement with a melody before listening to it. Lena stops: “That’s where I breathe.” The song becomes a negotiated collaboration. They draft the center proposal with separate fees for choreography and composition, a rehearsal budget, and a contingency performer. Zay is not in the scene.

The headline act / “A ROOM THAT HOLDS.” Velvet, Jude, and Lena work in the studio before a drag night. Jude’s spectacular flourish conceals a missed cue. Velvet stops the band and has him play underneath a held word until he understands the audience’s attention. A promoter later offers a much larger fee for an insulting, tightly controlled caricature. Andre says they will consider the offer; money has a legitimate pull.

The portrait / “WHAT THE LIGHT KEEPS.” Iris sketches Zay at the center. He asks to stand with the ball above his head. She asks him to sit and let his hands rest. He resists; she admits that her first proposal painted over everything difficult. Their duet makes looking reciprocal. Nearby, Vale conducts film study: the next pass exists before the defender makes it obvious. Zay begins to understand the same idea in two languages.

The joke and the missed call. Dani tries new material in a small comedy room and gets a huge laugh from Ben’s private history. Ben hears a recording later at home. His anger is not a theatrical walkout; he stops helping with the self-tape and asks for the clip to come down. Separately, Ben misses Iris’s scheduled installation because he said yes to another shift. Iris’s anger gives him no morally superior place to hide. Both scenes require an answer without music.

Credit / “COUNTERMELODY.” In Morgan’s workroom, Jude and Morgan build a cue. A four-note guitar phrase becomes the piece’s emotional center. Neither names the ownership question directly. Morgan receives a paid short-film scoring offer but conceals its deadline to avoid disappointing Lena. Zay arrives with an offer from a local sponsor: travel and camp lodging in exchange for a public profile film. Morgan agrees to supply a rough cue. “We made it together” is treated as sufficient permission when it is not.

The film / “IN THE FRAME.” Over several rehearsals, Zay gives the filmmaker material labeled for private rehearsal review and approves a cut that converts his friends into adversity around him. A vulnerable Lena voice memo about controlling her own movement is placed under club footage; Ben’s history is carried by Dani’s joke; Iris’s unfinished portrait is cropped into an emblem of heroic strength. Morgan licenses the cue before obtaining the necessary clearances. The film’s attractive presentation matters: Zay can see the version of himself he desperately wants to believe. Its “son who saved his family” framing flatters his sacrifice and erases Ray’s work. Zay agrees to that simplification before applying the same extraction to his friends. The film must not present addiction or Lena’s occupation as decorative evidence of his heroism without the drama exposing the harm.

At the center, a small screening coincides with the film’s public release. Early congratulatory notifications become objections in the room. Each principal sings a version of “That isn’t what I gave you.” Zay answers with “People are finally looking,” revealing the mistake rather than resolving it. The ensemble’s phrases compete, and the ball finds no place in the groove. A large projected face remains above eight separated bodies. Act I turning point: success as exposure has cost the support that made it possible.

Act II • November to December • the work after the promise

The morning after / “LOOK AGAIN.” Seven separate spaces, no tribunal. Lena cancels the next joint rehearsal. Iris writes a removal request. Jude mutes the guitar stem. Velvet calls the sponsor to reject use of the act. Dani calculates whether the new booking inquiry will vanish; Ben tells them calculation is not an apology. Morgan opens the cue agreement and sees exactly what they authorized. Zay first tries to explain intention, then reads the materials he ignored. He orders removal and posts a correction naming the affected work and its owners. August. Las Vegas, then New York City. The principals are adults. Sound, timing, and safety

The number is 144 bars of 4/4 at a grounded 96 BPM: approximately six minutes, allowing a few seconds for the opening breath. No accelerando. The feeling of acceleration comes from subdivision and overlapping voices. The final cadence is interrupted during bar 144.

Amplify the actual basketball with boundary microphones at the court surface, with enough isolation to prevent footfalls from becoming false ball attacks. Zay uses a playable, properly inflated ball. The drummer watches Zay and matches the first audible impact with a low kick; performer and drummer rehearse a shared visible downward cue. The ball does not follow a prerecorded bounce track. The musical director holds the opening pulse and adjusts within the groove if a bounce travels. The click, if used, supports the band and never makes the actor chase it.

The marked lanes are dry, free of cables, and checked before every performance. Guitar leads run away from them. Nobody crosses a dribble lane without the rehearsed visual cue. Movement can be adapted to the actor's basketball skill without changing Isaiah's intelligence or pleasure. No blind throw toward another performer or the audience is necessary.
