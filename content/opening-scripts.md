# 7. Complete opening sequence

## BOUNCE

*Working stage text and lyric draft. Music and choreography are specified for development, rather than represented as a completed orchestral score.*

### Performance frame

August. Las Vegas, then New York City. The principals are adults. ISAIAH “ZAY” REED is twenty-six and six feet eight inches tall, a Las Vegas native who has worked and saved to pursue his New York Knicks dream. The opening is a symbolic overture across distance: eight lives occupy a shared theatrical space before most have met. Zay’s literal home scene is in Las Vegas; the other seven live in New York. No exchanged glance establishes acquaintance. The journey after the household scene must clearly mark an overnight flight and the next evening’s New York arrival.

DENISE, Zay’s mother, is a server at Las Vegas’s fictional Desert Star cabaret and lives with alcohol addiction. RAY “UNCLE RAY” WALKER, her former husband, is not a blood uncle. Denise’s partners were routinely introduced to Zay as “Uncle”; Ray continued coaching and mentoring him after the divorce. Supporting actors or a clearly identified offstage voice may play the family roles. They are not additional chakra principals.

The stage contains eight practical working stations at its perimeter. A court-sized central lane and two crossing diagonals remain clear. BEN has a taped audition mark and phone. LENA has a portable barre. DANI has a microphone. VELVET has a coat stand and mirror. JUDE has an amplifier and guitar. IRIS has an upright working panel. MORGAN has a keyboard, manuscript, and pencil. ZAY owns the open floor. These are suggestions of places, not eight complete rooms.

Faces receive neutral front light. The distinct colored halos and side light are yellow for Zay, red for Ben, orange for Lena, blue for Dani, violet for Andre/Velvet, green for Jude, indigo for Iris, and pearl white for Morgan. The first seven draw on the familiar chakra palette. Soul Star, the pearl-white eighth identity, is this production's artistic extension. That information belongs in the program and design materials; nobody explains it in dialogue.

### Sound, timing, and safety

The number is 144 bars of 4/4 at a grounded 96 BPM: approximately six minutes, allowing a few seconds for the opening breath. No accelerando. The feeling of acceleration comes from subdivision and overlapping voices. The final cadence is interrupted during bar 144.

Amplify the actual basketball with boundary microphones at the court surface, with enough isolation to prevent footfalls from becoming false ball attacks. Zay uses a playable, properly inflated ball. The drummer watches Zay and matches the first audible impact with a low kick; performer and drummer rehearse a shared visible downward cue. The ball does not follow a prerecorded bounce track. The musical director holds the opening pulse and adjusts within the groove if a bounce travels. The click, if used, supports the band and never makes the actor chase it.

The marked lanes are dry, free of cables, and checked before every performance. Guitar leads run away from them. Nobody crosses a dribble lane without the rehearsed visual cue. Movement can be adapted to the actor's basketball skill without changing Isaiah's intelligence or pleasure. No blind throw toward another performer or the audience is necessary.

The repeating ball cell is two grounded attacks, a rest, then a quicker return. The useful action is the rest: Zay catches on it to sing sustained phrases. The same cell will later pass to feet, hands, guitar, and breath. Ball impacts are a musical voice, not continuous noise underneath every lyric.

### Bars 1–8 · 0:00–0:20 · Ball and kick

**BLACKOUT.**

A breath. A shoe settles on wood.

A concentrated **YELLOW SPOTLIGHT** rises on ZAY, entering with a basketball at his hip. He surveys the space like a defender he intends to fool. A little smile: he likes this part.

He drops his weight.

**His first dribble hits the floor precisely with a low KICK DRUM.**

He catches the return. Lets the silence hang. Another impact and kick. He tests the distance to the edge of his light, crosses over, returns. A sharp, economical change of direction earns him a laugh he does not ask for.

For the last two bars he establishes the ball cell. The kick answers rather than doubles every impact. He catches at his sternum on the final rest.

### Bars 9–24 · 0:20–1:00 · Zay's solo

A round bass note enters. Zay sings with the ball held; then resumes dribbling between phrases. His confidence is attractive and real, even if it is not secure.

**ZAY**

> They painted over half the key.  
> I know the missing line.  
> The rim leans left. I lean right back.  
> This crooked court is mine.  
>  
> Give me a hand that's closing fast,  
> Give me a second look.  
> You think you know which way I move—  
> You haven't read the book.

He sells a drive, plants, and slips sideways. A rim is unnecessary: the joy is in the choice, not a spectacular shot.

**ZAY**

> Down to the floor—  
> Up to my hand.  
> A little more sky  
> Than they thought I could stand.  
>  
> Don't hang my name up yet.  
> Leave room for what I do.  
> Give me the ball.  
> Give me a lane.  
> Give me a way through.

On “through,” he catches, looks up, and allows the bass to finish the thought.

### Bars 25–32 · 1:00–1:20 · Ben, red

A **RED HALO** reveals BEN at his audition mark. Soft rim percussion enters. He presses record, assumes a genial face, dislikes it, and starts again.

**BEN** *(spoken in rhythm)*

Ben Mercer. Six— No. They can see how tall I am.

*(He resets his own shoulders.)*

Hi. I'm Ben Mercer.

**BEN**

> Give me a room with a door that shuts,  
> A name on the chair by the wall.  
> One part I don't have to borrow—  
> Let me be there when you call.

Zay travels outside Ben's station on the upstage lane, never through his audition frame. Ben's final “call” remains audible after his red light settles.

### Bars 33–40 · 1:20–1:40 · Lena, orange

An **ORANGE HALO** reveals LENA at the barre. Brushed shaker joins. She executes an exact ballet phrase, checks a position in an absent teacher's mirror, then catches herself doing it. She removes her hand from the barre. The next phrase uses the same balance with a grounded hip, an audacious pause, and a teasing glance at the audience. Neither vocabulary is presented as the correction of the other. Both belong to her.

**LENA**

> Eight to get there, four to stay,  
> One to change my mind.  
> I can finish where you pointed—  
> I can leave that mark behind.

Zay's lateral slide and Lena's traveling step share a rhythm on opposite diagonals. They do not touch. Lena decides when her phrase ends.

### Bars 41–48 · 1:40–2:00 · Dani, blue

A **BLUE HALO** reveals DANI, testing a microphone. The cable stays in its station. A clipped keyboard chord answers the ball.

**DANI** *(spoken)*

My family says I make everything a joke. Which is unfair. Some things are invoices.

There is a beat in which Dani almost says something else. The smile gets there first.

**DANI**

> Give me seven paid minutes,  
> Give me people who stay.  
> If I tell it right, the terrible part  
> Can buy the train home today.

Zay pauses his dribble for the punchline. Then he continues through the cleared center as Dani tries a second microphone height.

### Bars 49–56 · 2:00–2:20 · Velvet, violet

A **VIOLET HALO** finds ANDRE halfway into VELVET SAINT's final sleeve. Velvet completes the transformation in full view: an exact adjustment of the cuff, a check of the hem, then a walk that changes the scale of the room. Organ and handclaps arrive.

**VELVET** *(to an imagined technician)*

The special follows me, darling. I don't follow the special.

**VELVET**

> Put my name above the doorway.  
> Leave my changes in the chart.  
> You can buy a seat this evening—  
> You don't get to buy the part.

Zay stops at the edge of Velvet's lane, gives the entrance its space, and rolls into a retreat dribble when the lane clears. It is a choreographic joke, not yet a relationship.

### Bars 57–64 · 2:20–2:40 · Jude, green

A **GREEN HALO**. JUDE plugs into a waiting amplifier. His live lead guitar gives a distinctive phrase: a bent note that seems to resolve, two quick descending notes, then an unexpectedly open sustained tone. The band leaves the gap around it intact.

**JUDE**

> Hear that note against the wire?  
> That's the one I came to find.  
> Let the room forget my fingers.  
> Let the sound stay in your mind.

He cannot quite resist embellishing the last measure. Zay slips behind him, safely outside the cable zone. Jude's riff becomes a counterline rather than a constant solo.

### Bars 65–72 · 2:40–3:00 · Iris, indigo

An **INDIGO HALO**. IRIS is painting on the upright panel. She lifts an immaculate little reproduction away from her working surface, studies the image underneath, and makes one broad, irreversible stroke. A mallet voice enters, dry and resonant. The brush is visible; its contact is not artificially amplified.

**IRIS**

> Somebody moved the window.  
> Everybody likes the view.  
> I remember where the light fell.  
> I can paint that shadow, too.

Zay crosses in front of the panel at a safe distance. She sees the interruption of light, chooses it, and adds another stroke. He does not yet know she exists.

### Bars 73–80 · 3:00–3:20 · Morgan, pearl white

A **PEARL-WHITE HALO** reveals MORGAN. Morgan listens, pencils a rhythm, shifts an orchestral voicing at the keyboard, then removes one note. The removal allows Iris's last syllable and Jude's sustained guitar tone to meet. The first full harmony appears.

**MORGAN**

> There's a place beneath your ending.  
> There's a word above that chord.  
> I can hear the song between us—  
> Let me put my name aboard.

Morgan starts to erase “my,” then sets the pencil down without erasing it. Zay dribbles around the station's outside edge and returns to center.

### Bars 81–96 · 3:20–4:00 · Eight working bodies

The groove opens into funk. No new lyric begins in the first four bars: all eight actions can be seen. Zay works a retreat, crossover, and forward drive; Lena answers with a change of level; Ben resets an entrance; Dani adjusts the microphone; Velvet snaps the cuff; Jude mutes a string; Iris lifts and commits; Morgan marks a beat. These gestures share weight transfer and timing without pretending they are interchangeable skills.

Across the next twelve bars, entries occur two bars apart. Keep each new voice forward in the mix.

**BEN**
> Give me a room—

**LENA**
> Give me the count—

**DANI**
> Give me a minute—

**VELVET**
> Give me the whole amount—

**JUDE**
> Give me a reason—

**IRIS**
> Give me the wall—

**MORGAN**
> Give me the credit—

**ZAY** *(catching the ball)*
> Give me the ball.

It lands as a joke and an admission. He spins the ball once in his hands, pleased with the rhyme.

### Bars 97–120 · 4:00–5:00 · Chorus

Full ensemble harmony, with soul warmth and gospel-rooted call and response. No generic wordless choir conceals an individual line. Zay sings the first call with the ball caught; the drummer carries its pulse. He resumes dribbling only during responses.

**ZAY**
> What do you do when the floor comes up?

**ALL**
> Bounce.

**LENA / BEN**
> Find your feet. Take a breath. Get your weight back down.

**ALL**
> Bounce.

**DANI / VELVET**
> We have worked all week for a little of this town.

**JUDE / IRIS / MORGAN**
> Let the window shake. Let the late train run.  
> There's a light still on where the work gets done.

**ALL**
> Bounce—  
> Off the wood, through the wire,  
> Through the blister and the rent.  
> We can tell you what it costs us.  
> We can't tell you what we're worth yet.

Eight bars of dancing carry the last line's discomfort rather than resolving it. Zay's pathway threads between the stations in a figure eight, each crossing released by a visible shift of weight. A demanding vocal pickup comes only after he catches the ball.

**ZAY**
> Put me where the light can find me.

**ALL**
> Let it find us working here.

### Bars 121–136 · 5:00–5:40 · Competing wants

The original short wants recur in an eight-part round, one entry every two bars. Ben's room, Lena's changing mind, Dani's paid minutes, Velvet's name, Jude's note, Iris's shadow, and Morgan's credit remain distinguishable. At first each tries to extend a phrase over the next. Morgan's hand draws the entries into a workable shape, but Morgan loses the end of their own line in doing so.

Zay returns to the central lane. The ball drives the whole room for four bars. He enjoys the power too much. His rhythm grows busier; other phrases have to squeeze around it. Lena finishes her own count anyway. Jude holds the open note instead of racing him. The room briefly finds balance by necessity.

**ZAY**
> Hear me coming. Hear me coming.  
> There is more of me than this.

**OTHERS**, individually becoming an ensemble

> There is more of me than this.

### Bars 137–144 · 5:40–6:00 · Almost arrival

The harmony lifts without a key change. Eight colors remain distinct. Zay takes the ball down the final lane, catches it on bar 141, and plants to sing. The other seven turn toward their own imagined futures, never toward him as a savior.

**ALL**
> Every fall has a rhythm.  
> Every hand can learn the ground.  
> If the world won't make a little room—  
> We will make a little—

**HARD CUT.**

No final chord. No button. No held tableau inviting applause.

### The household

The seven other lights disappear. The band is silent. A harsh overhead practical reveals a cramped Las Vegas apartment kitchen. A location cue reads **LAS VEGAS — AUGUST** and is gone. A bill lies beneath a supermarket magnet; a saucepan soaks in a sink too small for it. DENISE REED stands in work trousers with one shoe off, her DESERT STAR server’s apron slung over a chair. Beside a folded order pad are a glass and a partly concealed bottle. They are ordinary evidence, not a comic prop or a diagnosis delivered by lighting. Zay's caught ball is at his chest. The symbolic space has collapsed around his actual body.

**DENISE**

“YOU NEVER GIVE ANYTHING TO THIS HOUSEHOLD WHO THE FUCK DO YOU THINK YOU ARE? YOU THINK YOU ARE A BASKETBALL STAR? BRING SOME MONEY INTO THIS HOUSE”

Zay's fingers tighten on the seams. His jaw contracts. His shoulders move inward by perhaps an inch. At six feet eight, he has learned exactly how little room he is allowed to occupy.

The clock on the microwave changes. Nobody moves.

**DENISE**

I called you.

**ZAY**

My phone was in the locker.

**DENISE**

Of course it was.

She takes a folded notice from beneath the magnet and puts it on the table. It is real. She does not have to wave it.

**DENISE**

They're charging another late fee. I asked for more hours. Desert Star cut Friday.

Zay looks at the total. He puts the ball on a chair; it rolls toward the edge and he catches it before it falls. Even that little sound would be too much.

**ZAY**

I put a hundred and twenty in the account Tuesday.

**DENISE**

And how far did that get us?

**ZAY**

I've got forty-seven on me. The flight's paid for.

**DENISE**

Then change it.

**ZAY**

It's tonight.

**DENISE**

And the money you've been hiding?

**ZAY**

Tryout fee. A room deposit. I told you.

**DENISE**

You always put that first.

He starts a reply, then swallows it. She sees the swallow and hears defiance.

**DENISE**

Don't stand there looking down at me.

He lowers his eyes. The adjustment is immediate, practiced, devastating.

**ZAY**

I'm looking at the bill.

Denise sits. She presses a thumb into the arch of her bare foot. Without thinking, she folds a table napkin one-handed into a neat fan; work is still in her fingers. Then she pushes the glass behind the order pad. Her exhaustion and her skill are visible. Neither undoes what she has said.

**DENISE**

You said last month would be different.

**ZAY**

I know.

**DENISE**

I can't spend “different.”

He takes his wallet out. Counts thirty dollars onto the table. Keeps seventeen. Denise looks at the division.

**DENISE**

So you do have money.

The old argument is ready to begin another circuit. Zay recognizes the route. He picks up the ball instead.

From a hook behind the door he takes a small, worn bag packed for the journey: training clothes, an old Knicks cap, a thin document folder. He adds his charger and a clean pair of socks from the drying rack. The fare and tryout budget are in a separate account built from years of shifts; the forty-seven dollars is the cash in his wallet, not all his money. He has no confirmed bed in New York. There is no triumphant music. Packing is clumsy with the ball under his arm.

**DENISE**

What are you doing?

**ZAY**

Going out.

**DENISE**

Where?

He has no answer. He checks his pocket for keys twice.

**DENISE**

Fine. Walk away. You're good at that.

He stops at the door. The audience can see the speech he could make. He does not make it.

He opens the door. He leaves.

The door closes with an ordinary, imperfect latch. Denise remains at the table for one breath before her light goes out.

# 8. Complete transition and bar scene

## The journey to The Switch

**KEYS. LATCH. FOOTSTEPS.**

Zay stands outside the door, motionless. Through it, a chair scrapes. He starts walking before he can turn back.

The stairwell appears through two cold practicals. His bag knocks the handrail. He switches shoulders. A downstairs television sounds through a wall; a recorded laugh ends before he reaches it. He has to duck at the last flight's low pipe. His height is inconvenience now, not power.

At the building’s exit, a key strikes another key. Las Vegas traffic fills the space: service vehicles and the hiss of a bus, with distant entertainment spilling from a doorway. Zay’s steps settle into a slower version of the opening cell. A percussionist finds the keys’ small metallic interval, then stops when he stops at the crossing. The city does not obey him.

He wakes his phone. A prepaid flight confirmation is briefly legible: **LAS VEGAS → NEW YORK.** Beneath it, a separate note lists tryout fee, transit, food, room deposit. Saving enough to go has not made him safe when he arrives.

He calls **UNCLE RAY**.

**RAY** *(on the phone)*

You outside?

**ZAY**

Yeah.

**RAY**

Same corner. Five minutes.

A pause. Ray hears the breathing he has heard after bad practices and worse nights.

**ZAY**

She says you put this in my head.

**RAY**

You had a Knicks jersey before I had a key.

Zay almost smiles. Ray does not make that into a victory over Denise.

**ZAY**

You going back there?

**RAY**

No. I can take you to the airport. I'm not going back into that marriage.

Zay looks at the building. Ray allows the silence.

**RAY**

Still want the ride?

**ZAY**

Yeah.

**RAY**

All right. You got the whole game on that drive? Not just the buckets?

**ZAY**

Even the ugly ones.

**RAY**

Those are the ones a coach can use.

Two headlights open a narrow path. Ray enters only far enough to take the bag; he asks with a hand before Zay gives it over. A car door closes. The keys’ rhythm becomes an indicator click. Their goodbye can be played with two chairs, avoiding a realistic vehicle.

**RAY**

Dani know you're coming?

**ZAY**

Knows I'm landing. Doesn't know I'm asking for a floor.

**RAY**

Ask before you make it somebody else's plan.

Zay nods, not yet able to do it. Ray offers his fist. Zay meets it.

**RAY**

Wednesday. Send me the film if you want me to look.

The offer has a day and a task. It is not a promise to fix everything. Zay takes the bag. Ray’s light goes out as an airport announcement rises.

**OVERNIGHT FLIGHT.** A brief location-and-time cue reads **NEW YORK — THE NEXT DAY.** The hiss of cabin air becomes train brakes. The ball stays contained at Zay’s feet; no dribbling through an airport or transit car. A moving band of daylight crosses him, then evening streetlight. The audience must not mistake the journey for a walk between two neighborhoods.

Outside a New York station, he wakes his phone and types to DANI: **You still at Switch?**

Three dots. Then: **Second set at ten. Side door. Don't heckle. I'm fragile and insured badly.**

Zay almost laughs. He types **Can I—** and deletes it. He puts the phone away without asking for somewhere to sleep. Ray’s practical advice has not instantly changed him.

A bass note grows from the departing train. Brushes borrow his walking pulse. The music remains underscoring, with no new song. Zay arrives at THE SWITCH, a New York neighborhood bar and cabaret with a small raised stage, a proper rehearsal floor, and a back corridor shared with its dressing area. This is not the Desert Star where Denise works in Las Vegas. Dani is Zay’s childhood friend from Vegas, now building a career here.

Its noticeboard distinguishes separate bookings: comedy tonight, dance on another night, a live-music night, and Velvet's coming drag show. The place supports overlapping working lives; it does not stage every art form simultaneously.

Dani is finishing a set. Zay slips through the side entrance and stands where Dani can see him.

**DANI** *(to the room)*

My roommate bought a label maker. Now the milk says BEN. Which feels less like a boundary and more like we're drinking evidence.

Laughter.

**DANI**

You've been very kind. Please tip the people doing the work you can't interrupt with a microphone.

Applause. Dani steps offstage, clocks the bag, and lets the smile relax.

**DANI**

All the way from Vegas, and you brought luggage to my set. Finally, an honest review.

Zay gives half a smile.

**DANI**

Bad?

**ZAY**

Can I just be here a minute?

**DANI**

Yes.

Dani nearly adds a joke. Doesn't.

**DANI**

I've got ten minutes to argue about the money I already agreed to. Water's free. Put the ball under your chair, NBA.

**ZAY**

Not NBA.

**DANI**

I know. Neighborhood Basketball Acquaintance.

That gets him. Dani heads toward the manager. A bar worker gives Zay water after his small requesting gesture. He puts the bag between his feet and wedges the basketball beneath his chair.

LENA enters from the back corridor in street clothes, carrying a dance bag and a rolled rehearsal mat. She has returned for equipment after sharing an earlier rehearsal slot with Velvet. She is not performing tonight. She checks a note on the next dance-night schedule, makes a correction with her own pen, and notices Zay trying to fit both knees beneath a tiny table.

**LENA**

That one hates tall people. Take this one.

She indicates a table with a central pedestal, then sets her mat beside it. She is choosing to stay, not being assigned to tend him.

**ZAY**

You know the furniture personally?

**LENA**

We work together. Some of it's difficult.

He moves. She sits opposite, leaving him an easy way out of the conversation.

**LENA**

You always turn your foot before you sit down?

**ZAY**

Do I?

**LENA**

Little pivot. Like the chair's going to steal something.

**ZAY**

You always watch people's feet?

**LENA**

Better than watching their mouths. Feet usually tell you first.

He deliberately squares his feet. She laughs.

**ZAY**

Now I can't sit naturally for the rest of my life.

**LENA**

Sorry. Occupational hazard.

**ZAY**

You dance?

**LENA**

I do. Here some nights. Other rooms. Currently in a room with a radiator that only understands tropical weather.

**ZAY**

I play. Currently in a gym where the shower only understands winter.

**LENA**

We could exchange complaints. Balance the climate.

He shifts the basketball with his heel, then catches himself.

**LENA**

Don't stop. I like watching somebody do a thing they don't have to think about.

**ZAY**

That's the part you think about the most. So you don't have to.

She recognizes something precise. A moment of genuine attention.

**LENA**

Yes.

At the piano, the interval musician touches a soft chord while settling for the next set. A yellow side light finds Zay; orange warms Lena's side of the table. Faces remain natural. The room's normal activity recedes without disappearing. Neither is suddenly on the cabaret stage.

## AFTER THE APPLAUSE

## The duet breaks

**ZAY**

You learned all that here?

**LENA**

Some. Ballet first. Years of it. Then other things. The best floorwork teacher I ever had could open a beer with her shoe. Terrible trick. Incredible teacher.

**ZAY**

You still do ballet?

**LENA**

When I can get the room. I'm trying to make something that doesn't have to explain which kind of dancing it is before it starts.

**ZAY**

I'd watch that.

She accepts the compliment. Then he continues, intending generosity.

**ISAIAH**

“You’re too good for this place. You could be a real dancer again.”

The piano fails to complete its returning figure. Lena's hand stops on the table.

**LENA**

“Again?”

**ISAIAH**

“I mean, you could do something with your talent.”

**LENA**

“I just paid my rent with my talent.”

**ISAIAH**

“That’s not what I meant.”

**LENA**

“Then tell me what you meant.”

The bass leaves. The pianist keeps a single open interval, quietly, waiting for him as she is waiting.

**ZAY**

I mean a company. A proper stage. Where people come for the dancing.

**LENA**

What do you think people come for when I dance?

**ZAY**

You know what I mean.

**LENA**

I am giving you the chance to say it.

He looks toward the stage, then at the poster advertising a dance night. For the first time he notices what he had been able to ignore while enjoying her.

**ZAY**

It's just—if people see you like that, they don't see what else you can do.

**LENA**

You sat here for ten minutes. What did you see?

He cannot answer quickly enough.

**LENA**

Was I doing fine until you worked out where the money came from?

**ZAY**

No. I respect the work. You've got to do what you've got to do.

The open interval collapses to one note. Lena leans back, the physical opposite of their earlier shared space.

**LENA**

You keep telling me you respect it while you find another way to say I should be ashamed of it.

**ZAY**

I'm saying you deserve better.

**LENA**

Better pay? Better conditions? A floor that doesn't wreck my knees? Yes. The people who pay me being embarrassed to admit what I do? You brought that part with you.

Silence. The pianist takes both hands off the keys. The room has ordinary sound again: ice, a till, someone laughing too loudly at the far end.

**ZAY**

Look, I had a bad night. I was trying to say something good.

**LENA**

And now I have to help you feel good about saying it?

He catches his jaw tightening, the same gesture from home. He does not yet understand the repetition. The audience can.

**ZAY**

That's not fair.

**LENA**

No. It isn't.

She stands, takes the mat, and moves it out of his reach. It is a work tool, not a barricade. She lowers her voice before she raises it.

**LENA**

I want this conversation finished. Leave.

He begins to stand, but the need to repair his own image outruns his ability to listen.

**ZAY**

If you'd just let me explain—

**LENA**

“GET OUT. GET THE FUCK OUT!”

The room stops. She does not need to repeat herself. The bar worker opens a clear path to the side door. Dani, returning, sees Lena's face and does not make a joke or ask her to lower her voice.

Zay retrieves his ball and bag without touching her, reaching around her, or asking for one more minute. He leaves immediately. Nobody applauds Lena. Nobody plays a comic sting. She is shaking with anger, and angry about having to shake.

At the door, Zay almost looks back. He keeps going.

Outside, the city sounds larger than before. He stands with the basketball pressed against his hip. For a moment, habit suggests a dribble. He lowers his hand, but catches the ball before he releases it.

No kick drum comes to meet him.

**END OF SEQUENCE.**
